\begin{abstract}
Cloud infrastructure management increasingly depends on automated systems capable of detecting resource failures, predicting their onset before critical impact, and selecting appropriate recovery actions without continuous human intervention. This paper presents an explainable AI-driven autonomous cloud management platform that integrates ten sequentially connected processing stages: telemetry ingestion, anomaly detection, temporal buffer management, temporal feature extraction, multi-model failure-risk prediction, deterministic model-coefficient attribution, likely-failure-category inference, autonomous decision logic, simulated recovery, and post-recovery verification. The system is implemented as a deployable research prototype and evaluated on a controlled synthetic telemetry dataset covering eight distinct scenario profiles: two normal baseline profiles (stable and variable) and six failure scenarios (CPU spike, gradual CPU failure, memory leak, disk gradual exhaustion, network degradation, and combined gradual failure). Isolation Forest anomaly detection achieves precision 0.704, recall 0.826, and F1 0.760 on snapshot telemetry. Temporal Logistic Regression achieves mean F1 0.692 ($\pm$ 0.036) and ROC-AUC 0.813 ($\pm$ 0.021) across five random seeds, substantially outperforming both a majority-class baseline (F1 0.514) and a current-value threshold baseline (F1 0.156). Among 18 evaluated failure sequences, the model issued at least one high-risk prediction before labelled failure onset with a mean lead time of 18.06 timesteps. The XAI attribution layer computes exact coefficient-based contributions for each of 45 temporal features, enabling transparent identification of which telemetry patterns drive each prediction. A post-recovery verification mechanism re-evaluates simulated recovered telemetry using the same model, returning RECOVERED, NOT\_RECOVERED, or INCONCLUSIVE verdicts. All experiments are conducted on synthetic data; real-world deployment validation is identified as future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern cloud platforms continuously generate telemetry across CPU, memory, disk, and network resources. At scale, the volume of this telemetry far exceeds what human operators can monitor manually, and the time between failure onset and user-visible impact is often shorter than human response latency \cite{Lin2019AIOps}. These pressures have driven adoption of Artificial Intelligence for IT Operations (AIOps) approaches that automate detection, diagnosis, and, increasingly, remediation of cloud infrastructure faults \cite{Bogatinovski2022AIOps,Notaro2021AIOps}.

The dominant body of work in cloud anomaly detection addresses individual pipeline stages. Detection methods --- including Isolation Forest, LSTM-based log analysis \cite{Du2017DeepLog,Meng2019LogAnomaly}, and variational autoencoders \cite{Su2019OmniAnomaly,Xu2018Donut} --- achieve high accuracy on specific telemetry types but do not connect their outputs to remediation. Self-healing systems \cite{Samir2019SelfHealing,Jiang2020Mitigation} trigger recovery actions from threshold events but typically lack temporal prediction or explainability. Root-cause analysis methods \cite{Wang2018CloudRanger,Weng2018RCA,Chen2014CauseInfer} recover causal structure from distributed traces or dependency graphs but require instrumented infrastructure and do not include verification of recovery effectiveness.

The result is a fragmented landscape: capable individual components that are rarely evaluated as an integrated end-to-end workflow from telemetry ingestion through post-recovery verification. This paper addresses that gap by designing, implementing, and evaluating a prototype that connects all stages of the cloud management lifecycle as a single evaluated system.

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}

\subsection{Cloud Anomaly Detection}

Early work on cloud anomaly detection applied statistical and unsupervised methods to snapshot telemetry. He et al. \cite{He2016Logs} systematically evaluated six classical ML models on system log data, finding that simpler models achieved competitive performance on specific datasets. Xu et al. \cite{Xu2018Donut} introduced Donut, a variational autoencoder for seasonal KPI anomaly detection in web services, achieving F1 scores of 0.75 to 0.90. Su et al. \cite{Su2019OmniAnomaly} proposed OmniAnomaly, a stochastic RNN applied to multivariate server metrics, achieving 0.89 F1 on the Server Machine Dataset. Similarly, Li et al. \cite{Li2021InterFusion} proposed InterFusion, simultaneously modelling inter-metric and temporal dependencies for multivariate anomaly detection. These approaches demonstrate strong detection capability but output only anomaly scores without explaining which metrics or patterns triggered the alert.

Sequential log-based methods extend detection to event sequences. Du et al. \cite{Du2017DeepLog} proposed DeepLog, an LSTM that models normal log key sequences and detects deviations with F1 0.96 on HDFS logs. Meng et al. \cite{Meng2019LogAnomaly} introduced LogAnomaly, which combines semantic log templates with an LSTM classifier, achieving 0.98 F1 on BGL logs. Nedelkoski et al. \cite{Nedelkoski2019Tracing} applied LSTM classification to distributed traces in a microservice testbed with F1 0.95. A shared limitation among these detection-focused systems is their inability to explain which features or log patterns drive the anomaly score and to map these detections to concrete remediation actions.

\subsection{AIOps and Intelligent Operations}

Lin et al. \cite{Lin2019AIOps} surveyed the AIOps landscape at Microsoft Azure, identifying anomaly detection, root cause analysis, and automated remediation as the three central challenges. Bogatinovski et al. \cite{Bogatinovski2022AIOps} provided a structured taxonomy of AIOps techniques for cloud platforms, covering metric, log, and trace modalities. Notaro et al. \cite{Notaro2021AIOps} surveyed AIOps failure management, categorising methods into detection, diagnosis, and automated remediation streams. Aceto et al. \cite{Aceto2013CloudMonitoring} conducted a comprehensive survey of cloud monitoring systems, observing that most tools address anomaly detection but do not integrate predictive modelling or autonomous recovery.

\subsection{Explainability for Operational Systems}

Ribeiro et al. \cite{Ribeiro2016LIME} introduced LIME, which explains individual classifier predictions using locally fitted linear surrogate models. Lundberg and Lee \cite{Lundberg2017SHAP} introduced SHAP, a game-theory-grounded approach that assigns feature contributions consistent with Shapley values. Both methods apply to arbitrary model types but introduce approximation. For Logistic Regression, direct coefficient-based attribution computes contribution as (scaled feature value $\times$ coefficient), which is mathematically exact and computationally inexpensive. Li et al. \cite{Li2023XAISurvey} surveyed XAI methods for anomaly detection, categorising explanations as feature-based, model-based, and post-hoc. This study leverages model-based attribution, which is applicable because the integrated system employs Logistic Regression.

\subsection{Root Cause Analysis}

Wang et al. \cite{Wang2018CloudRanger} proposed CloudRanger, which constructs dynamic causal graphs from microservice dependencies and uses random walk to identify the root-cause service. Chen et al. \cite{Chen2014CauseInfer} proposed CauseInfer, a hierarchical causal inference method operating on performance metrics, correctly identifying root cause services in 82\% of cases. Weng et al. \cite{Weng2018RCA} applied the PC algorithm for causal discovery from cloud metric time series. These approaches require instrumented distributed tracing and explicit service dependency models. The present work uses a lighter-weight heuristic approach: XAI attribution strengths are aggregated by resource domain (CPU, Memory, Disk, Network) to infer the likely failure category. This does not establish causal proof but requires no tracing infrastructure.

\subsection{Self-Healing and Automated Remediation}

Samir et al. \cite{Samir2019SelfHealing} proposed a fuzzy-logic self-healing system for Docker Swarm microservices, reducing failure recovery time by 60\% compared with manual intervention, but relying on predefined rules without prediction or explainability. Gan et al. \cite{Gan2019Seer} proposed Seer, which predicts QoS violations in microservices from hardware telemetry with 91\% accuracy, enabling preemptive resource allocation, but without XAI or RCA. Jiang et al. \cite{Jiang2020Mitigation} demonstrated automated incident mitigation for cloud services by recommending troubleshooting guides, but without model-based explanation or post-recovery verification using the same model.

\subsection{Research Gap and Open Challenges}

The review in Section II reveals that the cloud management literature addresses individual pipeline stages well but rarely evaluates them as a unified end-to-end workflow. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Detection methods do not explain which features drove the alert.
\item XAI methods are applied to detection models but not connected to recovery actions.
\item Self-healing systems do not use temporal prediction and do not verify recovery with the same model.
\item Survey papers on Artificial Intelligence for IT Operations (AIOps) repeatedly identify system integration as an open challenge, yet they rarely provide integrated experimental evaluations.
\end{enumerate}

This work addresses these gaps by implementing and evaluating an integrated prototype that connects telemetry ingestion, temporal prediction, deterministic XAI attribution, failure-category inference, decision logic, simulated recovery, and model-based verification in a single system. While this system does not claim to be the first to combine cloud monitoring with self-healing, nor does it assert superiority over deep learning anomaly detectors, it provides a comprehensive end-to-end framework demonstrating a complete, interpretable, reproducible workflow in a research prototype setting.

\subsection{Research Questions and Contributions}

The specific research questions addressed are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How effectively can ML-based anomaly detection identify cloud resource failures across diverse multi-scenario synthetic telemetry?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Can temporal rolling features improve early warning of gradual failures compared with snapshot threshold monitoring?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Can model-coefficient attribution identify which temporal telemetry patterns most influence each failure-risk prediction?
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Can likely-failure-category inference and rule-based decision logic translate prediction outputs to appropriate recovery action selection?
\item \textbf{RQ5:} Can post-recovery re-evaluation using the same predictive model provide a meaningful verification signal within an integrated self-healing workflow?
\end{itemize}

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item An end-to-end integrated prototype connecting all ten cloud management stages in a single callable API.
\item A leak-free 45-feature temporal feature engineering layer using 10-step sliding windows.
\item A comparative predictive evaluation of five models across five random seeds with strictly sequence-isolated splits.
\item A deterministic logistic-regression coefficient attribution layer for XAI without approximation methods.
\item A post-recovery verification mechanism that re-evaluates recovery using the same trained model.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed System Architecture and Methodology}

The proposed methodology connects telemetry ingestion, anomaly detection, temporal feature extraction, multi-model failure-risk prediction, deterministic model-coefficient attribution, likely-failure-category inference, autonomous decision logic, and post-recovery verification as a unified sequential pipeline. The overall pipeline architecture is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_system_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Explainable AI-Driven Cloud Management System Architecture.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

The proposed platform comprises ten processing stages arranged as a sequential pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Telemetry Ingestion:} Synthetic generation of multi-metric sequences across CPU, memory, disk, and network interfaces.
\item \textbf{Anomaly Detection:} Snapshot point-in-time detection using an unsupervised Isolation Forest model.
\item \textbf{Temporal Buffer Management:} 10-step sliding window aggregation to accumulate historical temporal context.
\item \textbf{Temporal Feature Extraction:} Derivation of 45 leak-free statistical and regression features across trailing windows.
\item \textbf{Failure Risk Prediction:} Sequence-level classification using Logistic Regression to predict future failure probability.
\item \textbf{XAI Attribution:} Deterministic feature contribution computation via exact model coefficients and normalized values.
\item \textbf{Likely Failure Category:} Evidence score aggregation across resource domains (CPU, Memory, Disk, Network).
\item \textbf{Autonomous Decision Logic:} Rule-based selection mapping inferred failure category to targeted remediation.
\item \textbf{Simulated Recovery:} Mock execution of recovery logic (string selection representing remediation action).
\item \textbf{Post-Recovery Verification:} Model-based re-evaluation of simulated recovered telemetry using the same trained classifier.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Temporal Feature Engineering}

A leak-free temporal feature set comprising 45 features is constructed from each 10-step trailing window. For each of the five raw metrics, nine features are computed: current value, 5-step mean, 10-step mean, 5-step delta, 10-step delta, 5-step slope (via linear regression), 10-step slope, 5-step standard deviation, and 10-step standard deviation. The feature extraction module strictly utilizes trailing observations, ensuring no access to future values (thereby preventing data leakage). StandardScaler is fit only on training rows and applied consistently during live inference.

\subsection{Predictive Modeling and Target Formulation}

Logistic Regression was selected for the integrated system with configuration: C=1.0, max\_iter=2000, random\_state=42, class\_weight='balanced'. The prediction target is strictly forward-looking and binary: a label of 1 denotes that an active failure occurs within the subsequent 10 future timesteps ($t+1$ to $t+10$), strictly excluding the current observation at timestep $t$ to avoid target circularity, and 0 otherwise.

The model is trained at server initialisation on the Phase 3C seed-42 training split (4,920 rows from 120 sequences). The fitted scaler and model are used for all live inference. Logistic Regression is selected over Random Forest and Gradient Boosting not because it achieves the highest aggregate performance --- all three achieve similar F1 and AUC within measurement noise --- but because its coefficient-based attribution is mathematically exact and requires no post-hoc approximation method.

\subsection{Explainable AI and Failure Categorization}

\subsubsection{XAI Attribution}
For each live prediction, the contribution of feature $i$ is computed according to (\ref{eq:contrib}):

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:contrib}
\text{contribution}_i = \text{scaled\_value}_i \times \text{coefficient}_i
\end{equation}

where $\text{contribution}_i$ denotes the signed contribution of feature $i$ to the predicted log-odds of failure, $\text{scaled\_value}_i = (x_i - \mu_i)/\sigma_i$ is the $z$-score normalized feature value computed via the training-set StandardScaler, and $\text{coefficient}_i$ is the corresponding learned Logistic Regression weight. A positive contribution increases the predicted failure probability toward the high-risk threshold, whereas a negative contribution decreases it. The top 5 features by absolute contribution are reported for each prediction. This method is deterministic and computationally inexpensive.

\subsubsection{Likely Failure Category Inference}
Evidence scores for four resource categories (CPU, Memory, Disk, Network) are computed by summing absolute contributions of features belonging to each resource. The category with the highest summed evidence is reported as the likely failure category. This is a heuristic inference from model evidence, not a causal determination.

\subsection{Post-Healing Verification Protocol}

When predicted risk reaches HIGH or CRITICAL:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Pre-healing risk (score, level, label) is recorded.
\item The decision engine selects a recovery action (simulated string selection, not executed via cloud API).
\item A 10-step synthetic normal\_stable telemetry sequence is generated as a proxy for recovered state.
\item The same trained LR model evaluates this sequence.
\item Post-healing risk (score, level, label) is recorded.
\item Verification verdict determination:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{RECOVERED}: Post-healing risk level is evaluated as LOW ($\le 0.25$).
\item \textbf{NOT\_RECOVERED}: Post-healing risk level remains HIGH or CRITICAL ($\ge 0.50$).
\item \textbf{INCONCLUSIVE}: Post-healing risk level is evaluated as MEDIUM ($0.25 < \text{risk} < 0.50$), indicating partial remediation without achieving full baseline stability.
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

The temporal buffer is cleared after healing so the next polling cycle re-enters the WARMING\_UP state.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset Configuration and Splits}

The evaluation dataset encompasses eight distinct scenario types: two normal baseline profiles (\texttt{normal\_stable} and \texttt{normal\_variable}) and six failure scenarios (\texttt{cpu\_sudden\_failure}, \texttt{cpu\_gradual\_failure}, \texttt{memory\_gradual\_leak}, \texttt{disk\_gradual\_exhaustion}, \texttt{network\_degradation}, and \texttt{combined\_gradual\_failure}). Across these eight scenarios, a total of 180 sequences were generated and partitioned into 120 training sequences (4,920 supervised rows), 30 validation sequences (1,230 supervised rows), and 30 test sequences (1,230 supervised rows), yielding exactly 7,380 supervised rows under a 10-step trailing window and 10-step prediction horizon censoring ($60 - 10 - 10 + 1 = 41$ rows per 60-step sequence), as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:splits}.

\begin{table}[htbp]

Sequence-level splitting ensures no temporal window from a test sequence is visible during training. Zero sequence overlap is verified by an automated audit (evaluation\_audit.json).

\textbf{Positive label definition:} A timestep $t$ is labelled positive ($y_t = 1$) if an active failure is present in any of the next 10 future observations ($t+1 \le t' \le t+10$). The current observation at timestep $t$ is strictly excluded from the target calculation, ensuring that the model is trained exclusively on advance predictive indicators rather than instantaneous state detection. Positive samples constitute approximately 52\% of the dataset under this early-warning formulation.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection Evaluation (Phase 2)}

Isolation Forest is evaluated on snapshot telemetry. Baselines include a fixed-threshold classifier (alarm if any metric exceeds 80\%) evaluated on identical data.

\subsection{Temporal Predictive Evaluation (Phase 3)}

Five models are compared across five random seeds \{42, 123, 456, 789, 2026\}:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Majority-class baseline
\item Current-value threshold baseline
\item Logistic Regression (C=1.0, max\_iter=2000, class\_weight='balanced')
\item Random Forest (n\_estimators=100, class\_weight='balanced')
\item Gradient Boosting (n\_estimators=100)
\end{enumerate}

Performance metrics include accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC.

\subsection{Early-Warning Horizon Evaluation}

Among test sequences containing labelled failure regions, the number of timesteps by which the model issues its first HIGH or CRITICAL prediction prior to the labelled failure onset is measured.

\subsection{Explainability Evaluation Protocol}

Four representative XAI case studies are documented from test sequences spanning different scenarios. For each case, the top 5 contributing features, their directions, and the evidence scores are recorded.

Memory mean features show negative contributions in some cases, meaning that the model interprets high mean memory values as evidence against the specific failure label --- a reasonable behaviour when the model has learned that combined failures show different multi-resource patterns.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Temporal Features vs. Threshold Detection}

The results confirm that temporal rolling features substantially improve early-warning performance compared with snapshot threshold monitoring. The threshold baseline achieves recall of only 8.6\% (mean across seeds) because gradual failures accumulate below any fixed threshold until late in the sequence. Logistic Regression with temporal features achieves recall of 66.1\% (mean) with a mean lead time of 18 timesteps. This is the central empirical finding of the temporal component of this research.

\subsection{Model Selection Trade-offs}

Random Forest and Gradient Boosting achieve marginally higher mean F1 and AUC than Logistic Regression, but the differences are small relative to within-seed variance. The decision to integrate Logistic Regression is justified by interpretability: its coefficient-based attribution is exact, computationally inexpensive, and does not require SHAP or LIME approximation. This is a deliberate research design decision with a documented trade-off.

\subsection{XAI for Operational Context}

The coefficient attribution results show that variability and slope features are the strongest drivers of failure-risk predictions for gradual failure scenarios. This is operationally meaningful: an operator told that ``CPU 5-step slope is the strongest positive contributor'' can inspect the CPU trend chart and verify the attribution independently without relying on an unverified black-box approximation.

\subsection{Root-Cause Inference Limitations}

The heuristic for likely failure categories correctly identifies the dominant resource for most failure scenarios evaluated in the explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) case studies. However, it fails on sudden failures (where temporal features have not yet accumulated) and on combined failures (where evidence is distributed across all resources). These failure modes are documented and consistent with the broader temporal prediction limitations. The system does not claim causal diagnosis.

\subsection{Post-Recovery Verification Conservatism}

The INCONCLUSIVE verdict for network degradation is the honest and appropriate result. A system that claimed RECOVERED whenever risk decreased by any amount would overstate its recovery capability. The three-class verdict (RECOVERED / NOT\_RECOVERED / INCONCLUSIVE) enforces a conservative stance consistent with academic integrity.

\section{System Limitations and Threats to Validity}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Synthetic telemetry:} All data is generated by parameterised scenario profiles, not from real production systems. Generalisation to real infrastructure is unvalidated.
\item \textbf{Simulated recovery:} Healing actions are string selections, not cloud API calls. No real resource reallocation occurs.
\item \textbf{Sudden failure detection:} Abrupt failures produce the lowest F1 (0.529) because temporal trajectory signals are limited before onset.
\item \textbf{Root-cause inference is not causal proof:} Evidence scores aggregate XAI contributions by resource domain; this is a heuristic mapping, not mechanistic causal discovery.
\item \textbf{Post-healing telemetry is synthetic:} The RECOVERED verdict reflects model behaviour on synthetic normal telemetry, not real system recovery.
\item \textbf{Prototype model training at initialisation:} The integrated system trains the LR model at Flask startup; this is not production-appropriate.
\item \textbf{No real-world validation:} Validation on real cloud trace datasets (Alibaba Cluster, Google Cluster, Azure) is future work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper presented an integrated, explainable AI-driven cloud management prototype that connects telemetry ingestion, Isolation Forest anomaly detection, temporal feature extraction, comparative predictive modeling, deterministic coefficient attribution, failure-category inference, autonomous decision logic, simulated recovery, and post-recovery model-based verification within a single evaluated system.

The key experimental findings are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Isolation Forest achieves F1 0.76 on snapshot anomaly detection.
\item Temporal Logistic Regression achieves mean F1 0.692 and ROC-AUC 0.813 across five seeds, substantially outperforming threshold-based monitoring (F1 0.156).
\item The model provides early-warning predictions with a mean lead time of 18.06 timesteps before labelled failure onset.
\item Deterministic coefficient attribution identifies variability and slope features as the strongest predictive drivers for gradual failure scenarios.
\item The post-recovery verification mechanism conservatively returns an INCONCLUSIVE verdict when recovery is partial, thereby preventing false claims of successful remediation.
\end{itemize}

Future work will address the validation of these methods on real cloud infrastructure trace datasets, the replacement of simulated recovery with real orchestration API integration, the evaluation of multi-model ensemble attribution methods, and rigorous comparisons with deep learning temporal methods (e.g., LSTM and transformer networks) under identical experimental protocols.
